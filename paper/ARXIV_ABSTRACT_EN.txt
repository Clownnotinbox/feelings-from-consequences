Human decisions are guided by feelings, fast summaries of past consequences (Damasio's somatic markers). We ask whether a machine can grow such feelings from consequences alone, whether acting on them makes a robot safer, and whether a large language model (LLM) that makes decisions benefits from them. A preliminary study showed that feelings or answers set by the researcher make words tie with feelings, so we built a drone simulator in which every target comes from the physics of the world. By forking an exact copy of the simulator we label, for every moment and each of seven candidate actions, whether a contact follows within 2 s; homeostatic feelings (GPS distrust, being lost, low energy, strain) are labelled from the true state. A small recurrent feeling core trained only on these consequences stays calibrated in unseen places (ECE at most 0.041) and, used as somatic markers over candidate actions, cuts hits on the static world in closed-loop flight by 92% and 85% (familiar / unseen places, three seeds), against 42% and 22% for a classic reflex. In whole missions given as free text plus a rough point in real OpenStreetMap places, under wind, GPS loss and spoofing, and bird attacks, removing the core doubles the missions with a static contact on 500 paired missions and more than triples them on 300 fresh ones (14.7% vs 4.3%). The learned slow feelings are weaker: in hard missions an explicit energy-to-home rule over raw telemetry nearly halved battery failures in the air (16% vs 29%). The LLM results are mostly negative: feelings in words do not beat raw telemetry, words about the future are ignored, and a vision-language model does not single out a described target among look-alikes better than coordinates; only a small parser of the operator's request clearly helps. We release the system with a PX4 bridge tested in software-in-the-loop and list what a real aircraft still needs.
